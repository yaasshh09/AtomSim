1. Hydrogen's energy ladder and its light

Level: final year of school or first year of university.

  1. Open the energy levels for hydrogen. What is the energy of the n = 2 level, in eV? open in AtomSim
  2. How much energy does it take to free the electron from the ground state? Explain why this is the same number as the magnitude of the n = 1 energy.
  3. A photon is emitted when the electron drops from n = 3 to n = 2. Find its wavelength in the spectrum view, and say what colour it is. open in AtomSim
  4. The drop from n = 2 to n = 1 gives a much shorter wavelength. Find it, and say why you cannot see it.
  5. Switch the system to He⁺. Every energy is four times larger. Use the Bohr formula, E = −13.6 Z²/n² eV, to explain why. open in AtomSim
Answer key
  1. -3.40 eV.
  2. -13.60 eV is the ground-state energy measured from a free electron at rest, so freeing the electron takes exactly 13.6 eV: the ionization energy.
  3. 656.47 nm, red: Balmer alpha, the line that gives hydrogen nebulae their colour. The app quotes the wavelength in vacuum; tables often quote it in air, 656.28 nm.
  4. 121.57 nm, Lyman alpha, in the ultraviolet: shorter than the roughly 380 nm where human vision stops.
  5. Z = 2 for He⁺, and the energy goes as Z², so every level is 2² = 4 times deeper. The ground state is -54.42 eV.

2. Orbitals, sizes and nodes

Level: first or second year of university.

  1. Open hydrogen's 1s state in the radial view. What is the mean distance of the electron from the nucleus, in pm? open in AtomSim
  2. Now open 2p. How much bigger is it, on average? open in AtomSim
  3. Count the wiggles of the 4s radial function that cross zero. Check your count against the rule n − ℓ − 1. open in AtomSim
  4. Open 3d₀ in the 2-D cross-section and switch on the node marks. Which nodes are cones, and why does a cone show up as two straight lines? open in AtomSim
  5. Tilt the slice so that the 2p₀ orbital disappears completely. Which plane did you choose, and why is the orbital exactly zero there? open in AtomSim
Answer key
  1. 79.42 pm, one and a half Bohr radii: ⟨r⟩ = (3/2) a₀ for 1s.
  2. 264.73 pm, five Bohr radii and about three and a third times the 1s average.
  3. Three: n − ℓ − 1 = 4 − 0 − 1 = 3.
  4. 3d₀ has ℓ − |m| = 2 polar nodes, both cones about the z axis, at about 55° and 125° from it, where 3cos²θ = 1. A slice through the axis cuts each cone in two straight lines. 3d₀ has no nodal plane; that appears in 3d±1, where ℓ − |m| = 1.
  5. The flat plane through the nucleus, z = 0: normal along z, no offset. 2p₀ is z times a function of r alone, so it is exactly zero wherever z = 0.

3. Many electrons: what models get right and wrong

Level: second or third year of university.

  1. Open sodium with the Hartree-Fock model. Read its ionization energy from Koopmans' theorem and compare it with the measured value. open in AtomSim
  2. Do the same for helium. Is Koopmans too high or too low here, and why is helium different from sodium? open in AtomSim
  3. Under helium's ladder, find the correlation energy. What does Hartree-Fock assume about the two electrons that a correlated wavefunction does not?
  4. Switch off the exclusion principle for neon. Where do all ten electrons go, and what would that do to chemistry? open in AtomSim
Answer key
  1. Koopmans gives 4.95 eV; NIST measures 5.14 eV. Koopmans is low by about 0.2 eV, because removing the lone 3s electron barely disturbs the core, so the missing correlation dominates.
  2. Koopmans gives 24.98 eV against a measured 24.59 eV: too high. Removing one of helium's two electrons lets the other contract a lot, and freezing the orbitals ignores that relaxation, which outweighs the missing correlation.
  3. -1.14 eV. Hartree-Fock lets each electron feel the other only as an average cloud; a correlated wavefunction lets them avoid each other instant by instant.
  4. All ten fall into 1s. With no shells there is no valence shell, so the regularities of the periodic table, and with them chemistry, disappear.
